Disappearance of Water

A virtual group exhibition curated by Ian Wyatt

Mary Mattingly, “Limnal Lacrimosa,” 2021–2022 (detail)

Curatorial statement

I grew up assuming that water would always be there when I turned on the tap. Most people I know still live with that assumption, and until recently, I never questioned it. The inspiration for Disappearance of Water came from a talk with a friend who repeatedly described water in the past tense. It was about how hundreds of glaciers around the world are simply gone. It was about how the total amount of water on Earth stays more or less constant, yet it is steadily slipping away from the places where people and ecosystems depend on it. Glaciers and lakes are disappearing at an alarming rate and aquifers are being drained faster than rain can refill them. Even in places like Flint, Michigan, where water still flows, it has become unsafe to drink. This exhibition gathers nine artists who are observing these phenomena, and I hope their work lets viewers observe with them.

Water is one subject that is spiritual, universal, and environmental all at once. It is universal in a very literal way. Every human body is mostly water and no living thing (that we know of) can go without it. It is spiritual in nearly every culture on the planet. People are baptized in it, wash with it before prayer, pour it out as an offering, and build cities and sacred sites close to it. It is environmental because its disappearance is one of the clearest and most measurable signs of a changing climate. More than two billion people already live without safely managed drinking water. That figure does not even count the farmers, herders, and desert communities who watch their already scarce water supply shrink more each year. When water leaves a place, history, ritual, and even life leave with it.

So, why disappearance? I originally used "scarcity", but that describes an ongoing condition. While not inaccurate, I wanted to evoke the feeling, not of a condition, but of an event where there is a clear "before" and an "after". I wanted the exhibition to present a narrative that viewers move through in an order that emphasizes the idea that we are living in the midst of a disappearance event. The first section, The Source Recedes, looks at places where the loss has been visible and measurable for decades: melting glaciers. Then there are the enormous engineering projects that divert rivers to feed farms and the enourmous needs of cities and their populations. The second section, The Ruptured Commons, looks at what happens to bodies of water and to communities once that loss takes hold, from a vanishing inland sea to a poisoned city water supply. The final section, Living With Less, shows what daily life looks like after the water is gone, when people pump, haul, ration, and pay for what others receive almost for free.

What I most want viewers to take away is a sense that the disappearance of water is inexorable but uneven. It is happening from Montana to Morocco, From Michigan to Lebanon. It is happening in the Navajo Nation where they use very little water, and in the Utah suburbs just eighty miles away where they use as much as they like. The people who use the least water are often the first to lose it. I hope viewers leave with more awareness of the water they use each day, of where it comes from, and how and why it is distributed the way it is.

The Artists

Mary Mattingly. I placed Mattingly first because her work begins where so much of our fresh water begins, in mountain snow and ice. What drew me to her was her decision to make glacial loss a multi-sensory, immersive experience. Most climate imagery relies on before and after photographs, and I wanted the exhibition to open with something experiential, being surrounded by the sight and sound of the slow, unceasing march of glacial melt. She has worked with water systems for years, including her floating food forest Swale in New York, so I trusted her intent toward the subject.

Edward Burtynsky. I needed an artist who could show the sheer scale of human demand on water. Burtynsky has documented industrial landscapes more thoroughly than most. I chose him because his aerial views make the viewer complicit. We look down on the land the way engineers and planners do, and the beauty of the pattern masks the asymmetry and makes the damage easy to miss at first. His work also connects to the tradition of landscape photography, but turns it toward cause and consequence.

Susan Hoffman Fishman. I selected Fishman to mark the turn from cause to effect. Her series treats each shrinking lake almost like a portrait of an elderly loved one whose time is almost over. In some cases, they are more like a death mask. The scale of loss she captures gives context to the more human-focused works elsewhere in the exhibition.

Dala Nasser. I chose Nasser because she reminds viewers that losing water is a political event as well as an environmental one. Her installation is one of interaction with a specific body of water that has significant personal and political meaning. The river she works with crosses one of the most contested borders in the world. Her method of burying and soaking fabric creates portraits of place, created by the place. I was drawn to her idea of the river as a witness that has seen more than any human.

LaToya Ruby Frazier. Frazier’s work is the emotional center of the exhibition for me. I chose her because Flint shows that water can vanish from people’s lives even when it keeps running from the tap. Her long collaboration with the poet Shea Cobb and her family also models the kind of care I want this whole exhibition to show toward the people it represents.

Mark Tanous. I included Tanous, a personal colleague and life-long ceramic artist, because his work asks a simple question with complex consequences: who owns water? His story of a San Francisco street sweeper who watched public water get wasted and bottled made me think about how public drinking fountains have gradually disappeared in my own lifetime without me even noticing. His piece is tactile in a way that almost grieves the state of water accessibility.

Lucy + Jorge Orta. I chose the Ortas to open the final section because their sculptures explore the necessity of invention for survival in a world of water scarcity. They have spent many years making work about how water is distributed and who gets access to it. In the context of this exhibition, their piece connects the philosophical problems of the water crisis with the need for practical solutions.

M’hammed Kilito. I chose Kilito because his long project on the oases of southern Morocco shows what scarcity looks like for communities that have lived alongside the desert for centuries. He lives and works in Morocco and builds lasting relationships with the people he photographs. His closeness to and understanding of his subjects highlights the humanity of the struggle with water scarcity. His images are spare, hinting at a point in the future when there is almost nothing left.

Elliot Ross. I wanted the exhibition to end on a question. Ross lives between the Utah and Navajo Nation communities he photographs, and he includes himself in the problem rather than assuming a neutral, third-party perspective. I chose him because his work makes clear that history and laws shape who has and doesn't have water just as much as rainfall. This leaves the viewer with the question of where they stand.

I intentionally mixed artists who have shown at major museums and biennials, including Burtynsky, Frazier, Mattingly, Nasser, and the Ortas, with artists who are earlier in their careers or less widely known. The problem of water accessibility is a global one, so I chose artists from around the world; Canada, the United States, Lebanon, Morocco, Britain, and Argentina. Together, I hope they show that the disappearance of water is a single story told concurrently from many, widely dispersed places.
