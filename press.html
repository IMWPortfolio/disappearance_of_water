Disappearance of Water

Online exhibition

October 28, 2026

Participating artists: Edward Burtynsky, Susan Hoffman Fishman, LaToya Ruby Frazier, M’hammed Kilito, Mary Mattingly, Dala Nasser, Lucy + Jorge Orta, Elliot Ross, Mark Tanous

Curated by Ian Wyatt

Disappearance of Water, a virtual group exhibition, brings together nine artists whose practices document and reflect on the depletion of fresh water from the places that depend on it. With works of photography, sculpture, painting, installation, and ceramics, the exhibition views water as ecological, spiritual, and contested, observing its fate in glaciers, aquifers, pipes, and wells.

The exhibition is arranged as a three-part narrative. Part one, Where does it Come From, Where Does it Go? opens with Mary Mattingly’s Limnal Lacrimosa (2021–2022), an immersive listening room in Kalispell, Montana, in which collected snowmelt dripped into ceramic lachrymatory vessels. The tempo of the drips is determined by outdoor temperatures, transforming the gallery into a kind of water clock calibrated to the melting of the glaciers of Glacier National Park. It is followed by Edward Burtynsky’s Row-Irrigation, Imperial Valley, Southern California (2009), an aerial photograph in which a canal divides cultivated fields from open desert, calling the viewers' attention to the vast infrastructure of irrigation that supports agriculture and settlement across the United States.

Part two, In the Midst of Loss, considers what happens once that loss is irreversable. Susan Hoffman Fishman’s The Great Salt Lake Disappearing (2025), from her series The Tale of Lost Waters, combines satellite imagery, cyanotype, and acrylic into a piece that functions as both a geological record and a memorial. Dala Nasser’s Red in Tooth (2020–2023), presented at Sharjah Biennial 15, represents the Al Wazzani River through textiles buried along its banks and treated with rainwater and salt, producing a record of erosion, salinity, and pollution. LaToya Ruby Frazier’s Flint Is Family (2016), made in collaboration with the poet Shea Cobb and her family, documents the lead contamination of the water supply in Flint, Michigan, a city where water was plentiful, but not safe, a different kind of disappearance. Mark Tanous’s stoneware fountain The idea is the thing: Free Water (2022) presents the increasingly rare public drinking fountain as a weathered relic and as an argument for water as a free, shared resource..

Part three, Living With Less, turns to images of everyday life under scarcity. Lucy + Jorge Orta’s OrtaWater Chain Pump Unit (2023) joins a traditional hand-operated chain pump with filtration equipment, carafes, buckets, and the artists’ Antarctic Flag, proposing a sculptural necessity for collective survival. M’hammed Kilito’s Morocco-41 (2023), a piece from his long-term study of the endangered oases of southern Morocco, depicts a lone figure at a well set in open desert. The exhibition closes with Elliot Ross’s A Question of Balance (2023), a four-year project that contrasts Diné families on the Navajo Nation, many of whom haul water to their homes, with communities in Utah’s Washington County, who draw from the same Colorado River Basin, but at some of the cheapest rates in the country.

Somewhere among the landscapes, objects, and documentary photographs, Disappearance of Water asks viewers to understand water’s absence as a condition shaped by policy and history as much as by climate. Bringing together internationally recognized artists and emerging voices working across North America, Europe, North Africa, and West Asia, the exhibition explores the uneven nature of loss and the communities who bear it first, while asserting the necessity of sharing what remains.
