Artwork

Nine works in three sections, arranged to be viewed in order: from the source of the loss, through its effects, to the daily life that follows.

I. Where does it Come From, Where Does it Go?

Where the loss of water begins: in melting ice that might never return and in the systems built to meet a growing population’s thirst.

Mary Mattingly, “Limnal Lacrimosa,” 2021–2022

Immersive installation with glacial snowmelt, ceramic vessels, clear tubing, holding tanks, and sound. Kalispell, Montana.

I chose Limnal Lacrimosa to open the exhibition because it turns the melting of Glacier National Park into something a visitor can hear and feel. Snowmelt drips from tubing near the ceiling into ceramic vessels shaped like lachrymatories, the small bottles once said to hold mourners’ tears, so the room becomes a place of grief as much as a place of science. I am moved by the way the drips sped up and slowed down with the outdoor temperature, which means the artwork kept a record of the climate in real time over nine months. This piece sets up the first stage of my narrative, water leaving its source. Glaciers act as slow reservoirs that feed rivers through dry summers, and as they shrink, every community downstream inherits the loss. Mattingly’s title joins an old word for lakes with a word for tears, and that pairing gives the whole exhibition its emotional key. I wanted visitors to have a moment to reflect on the movement and sound of water, what it means to be surrounded by it, and eventually to imagine its absence.

Edward Burtynsky, “Row-Irrigation, Imperial Valley, Southern California,” 2009

Photograph

In this aerial photograph, a straight canal separates bright green fields on one side from bare desert on the other, and that single line tells the story of how the American West was made livable for millions of people. I selected it to represent the second cause in my narrative, the enormous and growing human demand for water. The Imperial Valley is one of the most productive farming regions in the country, and it depends almost entirely on water carried in from the Colorado River. Burtynsky’s high viewpoint makes the land look like an abstract painting, and I find that beauty unsettling because it hides how fragile the whole arrangement is. The desert on the right side of the frame reads to me as a preview of what returns when the water runs out. I placed this work beside Mattingly’s because together they show both ends of the supply chain, from melting snow to irrigated crops. It also introduces the Colorado River, which returns at the very end of the exhibition in Elliot Ross’s work.

II. In the Midst of Loss

What happens to lakes, rivers, and cities once the loss takes hold? What happens to the people who depend on them?

Susan Hoffman Fishman, “The Great Salt Lake Disappearing,” 2025

Acrylic, satellite images, cyanotype, and mixed media on paper, 70 × 26 in. From the series The Tale of Lost Waters.

This painting opens the second section of the exhibition, where causes turn into visible effects. Fishman layers satellite images, cyanotype prints, and paint into a tall scroll, and the deep blue of the lake appears as a broken fragment surrounded by rusty, cracked ground. The scroll format reads like a family record or a memorial, and the Great Salt Lake deserves to be remembered in both ways. The lake has lost a large share of its water over recent decades as the rivers that feed it are diverted to farms and cities, which links it directly to Burtynsky’s canal. I also think of Robert Smithson’s Spiral Jetty, built into this same lake in 1970 and now stranded on exposed lakebed, so the painting carries a piece of art history with it. Across this series Fishman uses medical gauze to suggest the wounds inflicted on bodies of water, and that idea of land as an injured body runs through the rest of the show. I wanted a painter’s hand at this point to slow the viewer down after an installation and a large photograph.

Dala Nasser, “Red in Tooth,” 2020–2023

Fabric buried in earth, washed in rainwater, and boiled in salt water; steel cables; coral stones; with an accompanying video. Installation view, Sharjah Biennial 15.

Nasser’s installation follows the Al Wazzani River in southern Lebanon. I selected it because it shows that disappearing water is tied to borders, conflict, and land. Nasser buries her fabrics near the river's banks, washes them with collected rainwater, and boils them in salt water, so the river’s pollution and rising salinity stain and dye the cloth. I find this process deeply spiritual, like a ritual of listening to the ground. Hung from cables that trace the river’s path across the courtyard, the fabrics become a map that visitors follow. Her work gives this exhibition a voice for the land itself and for the animals and plants that also depend on the river. It also helps the narrative shift from the shrinking of water to the damage done to the water that remains. Its placement in the exhibition just before Frazier’s photograph is a reflection on the damage being done to existing water supplies.

LaToya Ruby Frazier, “Flint Is Family,” 2016

Photograph

Frazier spent months in Flint, Michigan, in 2016 with the poet Shea Cobb and her family, recording how they lived through the city’s lead-contaminated water crisis. I selected this work because it expands what disappearance can mean: in Flint, water still came out of the tap, yet families could no longer trust it, so in every practical sense it was gone. The image shows a crowd gathered around a barrel marked with a biohazard symbol, with a figure in a white protective suit and a child standing among the adults, and I find that the combination is hard to look away from. The crisis began in 2014 when officials switched the city to a new water source to save money, which shows that disappearance can have many causes, including budget and policy decisions. Frazier’s collaborative approach with the people she photographs reflects the ethic I wanted to carry throughout this exhibition. Placing her photograph at the center of the exhibition keeps the people affected by water loss at the center as well.

Mark Tanous, “The idea is the thing: Free Water,” 2022

Stoneware and found hardware

Tanous’s stoneware fountain, with two old faucets, a small basin, and a cast plate that reads “WATER!” looks like something pulled from a city street after decades of exposure to the elements. I included it because it asks who owns water at a time when free public drinking fountains have largely disappeared, replaced by bottled water sold for profit. The work grows out of the story of R. J. Jenks, a San Francisco street sweeper who watched water get wasted and locked away, and Tanous builds his pieces the way Jenks gathers what other people throw out. I love that the fountain looks both precious and broken, as if it has already been dug up as a relic from a time when water was free. Tanous brings a handmade warmth to a show dominated by photographs and installations. His piece closes the middle section by turning attention from environmental loss to economic forces. Placing it right after Flint makes his call for free water feel urgent and practical.

III. Living With Less

When there's no longer enough water, who gets what remains, and how?

Lucy + Jorge Orta, “OrtaWater Chain Pump Unit,” 2023

Steel structure, traditional manual hydraulic wheel chain pump, water filtration unit, canteens, bivouacs, glass carafes, Antarctic Flag, buckets, and various water containers.

I wanted the Ortas’ sculpture to open the final section because it imagines how people might live once water has to be pumped, processed, and shared by hand. The piece joins a wooden chain pump, a tool still used for irrigation in some parts of the world, with a filtration unit, glass bottles,and buckets, all mounted on a steel frame. Above it flies the artists’ earlier piece, Antarctic Flag (2007), which blends together the flags of many nations, suggesting communal stewardship of water resources. I am drawn to the way the sculpture is both a museum object and a working machine, like putting survival gear on an altar. In the pictured installation it stands in front of an ornately decorated wall. That contrast between necessity and luxury foreshadows the gap between communities we explore in the final two pieces. The Ortas have worked with questions of water and access for many years. This piece connects the problems shown earlier in the exhibition to the harsh realities that end it.

M’hammed Kilito, “Morocco-41,” 2023

Photograph, 100 × 100 cm. From the series Before It’s Gone.

In this photograph from Kilito’s project on Morocco’s oases, a man in white kneels, peering into a concrete well in the middle of what appears to be open desert. I selected it because it shows, with almost nothing else in the frame, what daily life looks like once the water is no longer plentiful. Oases in southern Morocco have protected communities from the desert for centuries, but drought, overuse of groundwater, and climate change have dried out many of them. Many young people have left as a result. Kilito lives and works in Morocco, and I value that his images come from close relationships with the people he photographs. The red metal frame and green rope stand out sharply against the pale dunes. In the context of the photo, the well itself gains a sculptural quality, which echoes the Ortas' piece above. I also see a spirituality in the man’s posture, which resembles a moment of prayer. This image narrows the exhibition's narrative from broad systems and policies to a single person.

Elliot Ross, “A Question of Balance,” 2023

Analog photograph, medium format

Ross’s photograph closes the exhibition because it gives the most direct picture of living with scarcity in the United States. Blue water barrels line the ground outside a small building beneath desert rock spires on the Navajo Nation, where more than one in three Diné residents must haul water to their homes. Ross’s project compares these families with residents of Washington County, Utah, who draw on the same Colorado River Basin, pay some of the lowest water rates in the country, and use the most water per person. Ross lives between these two communities, and I respect that he places himself inside the question. I ended here because his work makes clear that water disappears according to history, law, and power as much as climate, which brings Burtynsky’s canal from the opening back into view. The project includes the words of rancher Billie Charlie, who asked that people come before corporations and that balance come first, and I wanted that thought to be the last one visitors carry. After moving from glacier to well, the viewer arrives at a choice about how the water that remains will be shared.
